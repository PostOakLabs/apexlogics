AL-92 · Selected Studies — Economics

Comparative Economic Systems Explorer

Every economy answers three fundamental questions: What goods and services should be produced? How should they be produced? For whom should they be produced? Market economies, centrally planned economies, and mixed economies give radically different answers — and 20th-century history ran the experiment. This tool maps the three systems across institutional and allocative dimensions, then walks through four case studies that show how the theory played out in practice.

Data vintage: Framework: Gregory & Stuart (2004), Comparing Economic Systems in the 21st Century; Samuelson (1948), Economics; case study data compiled from historical sources
1. What to Produce?
Market Economy
Consumer preferences (expressed through purchasing decisions) + profit signals tell producers what to make. If consumers want it and pay for it, firms produce it. Entrepreneurs who correctly anticipate demand earn profits; those who misjudge lose capital and exit.
Centrally Planned
Central planners decide based on stated social priorities (heavy industry, military, basic needs). Gosplan in the USSR set output targets for thousands of products. Consumer preferences were subordinate to plan objectives — leading to chronic shortages of consumer goods alongside surpluses of heavy industrial output.
Mixed Economy
Mostly market signals, but government steers investment in public goods (defense, infrastructure, basic research), corrects externalities (pollution taxes, safety regulation), and ensures provision of merit goods (education, healthcare) that markets tend to underprovide.
2. How to Produce?
Market Economy
Firms compete to minimize costs. Technology adoption is driven by the profit motive — firms that adopt more efficient production methods survive; inefficient producers exit. The price system coordinates input allocation without central direction (Hayek's "spontaneous order").
Centrally Planned
A central planning bureau allocates inputs (materials, labor, capital). Without price signals, measuring efficiency is extremely difficult — Hayek's "knowledge problem" in full display. Planners lack the decentralized local information that prices encode, leading to systematic misallocation.
Mixed Economy
Mostly private production with market competition; state enterprises in strategic sectors (energy, defense, infrastructure); regulation sets minimum standards (safety, environment, labor). Public sector production tends to be less efficient but may serve equity or strategic goals markets underweight.
3. For Whom to Produce?
Market Economy
Distribution follows ability to pay. Those with higher incomes consume more. Income is determined by labor market wages + returns to capital ownership. Market distribution reflects productivity and factor ownership, not needs — producing extreme inequality without redistribution.
Centrally Planned
Distribution follows plan priorities. Housing, healthcare, and basic food may be heavily subsidized. Income differences were compressed (but not eliminated — party members and technical elites had significant privileges and access to special distribution channels).
Mixed Economy
Market distribution + redistribution through taxation and transfers (Social Security, Medicare, SNAP in the US; NHS, universal basic pension in the UK). Degree of redistribution varies enormously by country — Nordic states achieve Gini of ~0.27; US market Gini is ~0.50 (pre-tax/transfer).
