Survey Study Designer

A well-designed survey study is harder than it looks. This guided workflow walks through the five stages of survey research design — from sharper research questions through sampling strategy, instrument design, pilot planning, and analysis plan. Grounded in the methodology of a 2005 study on performance-enhancing supplement use among student-athletes at a Division I university (n=226, SPSS analysis), which demonstrated the messiness of real research design.

📊 Data: Survey methodology: Fowler (2014), Survey Research Methods (5th ed.); Dillman et al. (2014), Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed-Mode Surveys; Likert (1932) scale origins

Stage 1 — Research Question
Write your initial draft — we'll sharpen it next.
Quality Checklist
RQ Sharpener — Example Transformations
❌ Vague / untestable
"Do athletes use supplements?"
✓ Specific / empirically testable
"What is the self-reported prevalence of dietary supplement use among NCAA Division I student-athletes at [University X], and what are the primary perceived reasons for use?"
❌ Too broad
"How does coaching affect athletes?"
✓ Bounded and measurable
"Is there a statistically significant relationship between perceived coach pressure (5-point Likert scale) and self-reported frequency of dietary supplement use among Division I athletes?"
❌ Normative (not empirical)
"Should student-athletes use performance-enhancing supplements?"
✓ Descriptive / comparative
"Do Division I athletes who receive coach-provided nutrition guidance report lower rates of unsanctioned supplement use than those who do not?"

The full group your research question is about.

Sampling Strategy
Simple Random Sampling
Equal probability for all; requires complete roster.
Stratified Random
Sample within subgroups; ensures representation.
Cluster Sampling
Sample natural groups (teams, schools); less precise.
Convenience / Purposive
Whoever is available; fast but limits generalizability.
Systematic Sampling
Every nth person from a list; simple and practical.
Sample Size Estimator
Total accessible population
±% acceptable error
Required n

Formula Used
n₀ = Z² × p × (1–p) / e²
n = n₀ / (1 + (n₀–1)/N)
Z=z-score, p=0.5 (max variance), e=margin of error
Nonresponse bias note: Nonresponse is not random. Who doesn't respond tells you something. Plan for it: use incentives, send reminders, and compare early vs. late responders to test for response bias. A 40% response rate with nonresponse bias can be worse than a 70% response rate without it.
Stage 3 — Instrument Design
Likert Scale Builder
5-Point Agree/Disagree
1 — Strongly Disagree
2 — Disagree
3 — Neither agree nor disagree
4 — Agree
5 — Strongly Agree
Use for: attitude/opinion items. Neutral midpoint is genuinely useful here.
5-Point Frequency
1 — Never
2 — Rarely
3 — Sometimes
4 — Often
5 — Always
Use for: behavioral frequency items. Avoid "always" anchors for behaviors that vary.
7-Point Intensity Scale
1 — Extremely Low
4 — Moderate
7 — Extremely High
Use for: measuring degree of construct (e.g., perceived pressure). More variance than 5-point.
Semantic Differential
Harmful — 1 2 3 4 5 — Beneficial
Weak — 1 2 3 4 5 — Strong
Use for: bipolar constructs (good/bad, strong/weak). Osgood's original scale.
Item Writing Checklist (per question)
Sample Question Bank
Supplement Use (Frequency)
Behavioral Frequency · 5-Point Scale"How often do you use dietary supplements (protein powders, creatine, vitamins, etc.)? 1=Never, 2=Rarely, 3=Sometimes, 4=Often, 5=Always"
Type Checklist · Binary"Which of the following supplements have you used in the past 12 months? (Check all that apply): □ Protein powder □ Creatine □ Multivitamins □ Energy drinks □ Pre-workout formulas □ None"
Perceived Pressure (Likert)
Coach Pressure · 5-Point Intensity"To what extent do you feel pressure from coaches to improve athletic performance through nutrition or supplementation? 1=No pressure at all, 5=Extreme pressure"
Peer Influence · 5-Point Agree/Disagree"Most of my teammates use dietary supplements." (SA/A/N/D/SD)
Knowledge / Awareness
True/False Knowledge Item"Creatine is classified as a banned substance by the NCAA. □ True □ False □ Not sure" (Correct answer: False — creatine is not banned by NCAA)
Knowledge Scale Item"How familiar are you with the NCAA's banned substance list? 1=Not at all familiar, 5=Extremely familiar"

Stage 4 — Pilot Plan

      

        Cognitive Interviewing: Test 5–7 people from your target population. Ask them to think aloud while completing the survey. What are they actually interpreting? Pay attention to: how long they pause on specific questions, when they ask for clarification, and whether their verbal reasoning matches the intent of the item.

Stage 5 — Analysis Plan

      
Always report descriptive statistics before inferential tests. Readers need to know means, SDs, and n before they can interpret a p-value.

      
Descriptive Statistics to Report

      
    
        
  • Frequencies and percentages for all categorical/nominal variables (sport, gender, year in school)
    • 
        
  • Mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) for each Likert scale item
    • 
        
  • M and SD for composite scale scores (sum or mean of items within a construct)
    • 
        
  • Range (min–max) and n per item (flag missing data)
    • 
        
  • Cronbach's alpha (α) for multi-item scales — reliability check (α ≥ 0.70 acceptable; ≥ 0.80 preferred)

APA Reporting Templates

      
t-test

      
An independent samples t-test was conducted to compare [outcome] for [Group A] (M = ___, SD = ___) and [Group B] (M = ___, SD = ___). There was a [significant / no significant] difference; t(df) = ___, p = .___, d = ___.

      
Chi-square

      
A chi-square test of independence revealed a [significant / no significant] association between [Variable 1] and [Variable 2], χ²(df, N = ___) = ___, p = .___, Cramér's V = ___.

      
Pearson Correlation

      
There was a [positive / negative / no] correlation between [Variable A] and [Variable B], r(n–2) = ___, p = .___. [Variable A] accounted for ___% of the variance in [Variable B].

      
Missing Data Plan

      
Report n per item in your results table. Consider listwise vs. pairwise deletion strategy. Mean imputation is acceptable for <5% missing on any given item. If missing data exceeds 10%, report it explicitly and discuss potential nonresponse bias. Never simply drop cases without acknowledging the implications for your sample.
