Counterfactual Stress-Tester

What if Napoleon had won at Waterloo? What if the South had won the Civil War? Counterfactual history is controversial but analytically valuable when done rigorously. This tool forces evidence-constrained counterfactual reasoning through five structured steps — ensuring your "what if" stays grounded in what was historically possible and what the actual consequences would have been, not just what we wish had happened.

📊 Counterfactual methodology: Ferguson (1997), Virtual History; Tetlock & Belkin (1996), Counterfactual Thought Experiments in World Politics; Lebow (2010), Forbidden Fruit

Define Your Counterfactual Premise Ferguson's Rule
Quick-Fail Checklist All three must be YES to proceed

These are the basic validity conditions for a well-formed counterfactual. If you cannot check all three, the counterfactual may be too speculative to be analytically useful.

This event is historically contingent — it could plausibly have gone differently given different actions or circumstances.
The counterfactual is close in time and space to the actual event (not a distant, sweeping alternative history).
I am willing to hold other structural factors constant where possible (minimal rewrite principle).
Step 1 of 5
Evidence Constraint Check
What actual historical evidence constrains what could have happened? Even in your counterfactual world, structural forces, economic trends, and demographic realities still apply.
What structural forces (economic, demographic, geopolitical) were operating independent of your changed event?
Which outcomes were probably inevitable regardless of your counterfactual?
What was the minimum change needed to produce your counterfactual?
Step 2 of 5
Nearest Possible World (NMW)
Describe the minimal change from the historical record. Ferguson's rule: change as little as possible from the actual to make the counterfactual true.
Bad NMW: "If the Roman Empire had never existed" — too massive a counterfactual, no specific point of divergence.
Good NMW: "If Franz Ferdinand's motorcade had taken the planned route and avoided Gavrilo Princip entirely" — minimal, specific, plausible.
Step 3 of 5
First-Order Effects
What would immediately and directly result from your changed event, within the next 6–12 months? Stay as close to the evidence as possible.
Staying as close as possible to the evidence, what would have been different in the next 6–12 months?
Step 4 of 5
Second-Order Effects
Trace the consequences of your first-order effects. Be honest about confidence levels — the further you project, the more speculative.
Warning: The further you project, the more speculative. Mark high-confidence vs. low-confidence claims explicitly.
1 = highly speculative, 5 = well-supported by historical analogy
Step 5 of 5
Magnitude & Calibration
How big was this event's causal contribution, really? Select the best description of the event's causal role.
Essential
Without this event, nothing else would have caused the same outcome. It was causally irreplaceable.
Highly Probable Trigger
This event made the outcome ~80% more likely. Without it, the outcome was possible but unlikely.
Accelerant
The outcome was likely anyway; this event simply sped it up or determined its specific form.
Contingent Trigger
This particular path was one of several equally likely triggers. Another event would have served the same causal role.
Five Counterfactual Fallacies Flag those present in your argument

These are the most common errors in counterfactual reasoning. Check any that you detect in your own argument — then go back and address them.

1. Inevitable Outcome Fallacy
"Even without X, Y would have happened." This is often used to dismiss counterfactuals entirely rather than engage with them. Sometimes it is correct — structural forces really do make certain outcomes nearly inevitable. But it is frequently an evasion that substitutes teleological thinking for genuine causal analysis. The burden of proof is on the person claiming inevitability.
2. Great Man Fallacy
Overweighting individual decisions and underweighting structural forces. WWI is the classic case: would WWI really not have happened without Gavrilo Princip? The structural tensions — imperial rivalry, arms race, alliance system, mobilization timetables — made major European conflict very likely by 1914. Princip was the trigger, not the cause. Counterfactuals that hinge entirely on individual choices without accounting for structural forces are usually weak.
3. Domino Fallacy
"If X had happened, everything good/bad today would be different." This overestimates the butterfly effect in history. Historical systems have enormous resilience and self-correcting tendencies. Most single events — even significant ones — do not cascade into total reconfigurations of history. Second- and third-order effects become increasingly uncertain, and intellectual honesty requires acknowledging this uncertainty rather than projecting confident chains of consequences indefinitely into the future.
4. Anacyclosis Fallacy
Using the counterfactual to confirm a pre-existing ideological view, regardless of the evidence. Example: "Of course the South would have freed the slaves eventually even without the Civil War" — an argument that serves a particular political purpose (minimizing the war's necessity) without engaging rigorously with the actual historical evidence (which strongly suggests the opposite). When a counterfactual conveniently confirms your priors, that is a warning sign.
5. Nearest Possible World Inflation
Changing too many variables at once, making the counterfactual untethered from any actual historical moment. Good counterfactuals change one specific thing and hold as much else as possible constant. When a counterfactual requires imagining a fundamentally different world — different technology, different culture, different institutions — it has moved from historical counterfactual into alternative universe fiction. That can be fun, but it is not historical reasoning.
Three Well-Formed Counterfactuals Ferguson 1997

These examples show how to construct a rigorous counterfactual: minimal change, explicit evidence constraints, graduated confidence, and honest calibration about magnitude.

"What if the Spanish Armada had succeeded in 1588?"
Evidence Constraints
Spain had military superiority on paper but serious logistical weaknesses: the plan required perfect coordination between Parma's land army and the Armada, which was never achieved. English gunnery technology was competitive. The storm that dispersed the fleet was historically contingent — but weather in the English Channel in August is structurally unfavorable. NMW: calm weather and Parma's army makes the rendezvous.
First-Order Effects
Spanish/Catholic army lands in England; Elizabeth I's government collapses or is captured; English Protestant establishment faces immediate existential threat.
Second-Order Effects (moderate confidence)
Spanish control of England would have been difficult to maintain — Protestant resistance movements, financial strain, over-extension of Spanish Empire. Long-term Spanish hegemony over England is unlikely. More plausible: a negotiated Catholic restoration under a Spanish client king, Protestant exile communities organizing resistance. The Dutch revolt would have been affected. English colonization of America would have been delayed but not necessarily eliminated.
Magnitude Assessment
Accelerant / Contingent Trigger: The structural forces driving Atlantic trade, Protestant-Catholic conflict, and eventual English colonial expansion were real and would have found expression through other channels. The Armada's failure was decisive for the specific timing and form of English dominance, not necessarily its ultimate occurrence.
"What if Lincoln had survived to oversee Reconstruction?"
Evidence Constraints
Lincoln's second inaugural ("with malice toward none") signals a moderate Reconstruction policy. But structural constraints were severe: Southern white resistance, Northern war fatigue, the limits of federal administrative capacity in the 1860s. NMW: Booth fails at Ford's Theatre on April 14, 1865.
First-Order Effects
Lincoln oversees readmission of Southern states under his Ten Percent Plan rather than the Radical Republicans' more stringent Wade-Davis conditions. Johnson's catastrophic vetoes of Civil Rights legislation don't happen. The Freedmen's Bureau receives more executive support.
Second-Order Effects (lower confidence)
The structural forces underpinning white supremacy in the South — land ownership patterns, Black Codes' logic, Northern business interests — were powerful and not dependent on Johnson. A Lincoln Reconstruction might have been more successful than Johnson's but still faced enormous resistance. The 14th and 15th Amendments might have been more robustly enforced. But the "Redemption" movement's eventual triumph reflects structural realities that Lincoln's presence would not necessarily have overcome.
Magnitude Assessment
Highly Probable Trigger for improved short-term outcomes, but likely Accelerant/Contingent for the long-term failure of Reconstruction. Johnson made a bad situation catastrophically worse; Lincoln would have done better; but whether Lincoln could have overcome structural Southern resistance is genuinely uncertain.
"What if Stalin had died in 1941?"
Evidence Constraints
In 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa, June 22). Stalin was briefly paralyzed by the invasion. The Soviet state was genuinely in crisis. NMW: Stalin dies of a stroke in early July 1941, when the military situation was catastrophic. Structural constraint: the Soviet military-industrial base and the Red Army's organizational capacity existed independent of Stalin's personal control, though Stalin had created the conditions for the 1941 disasters through his purges.
First-Order Effects
Succession crisis in Moscow during the worst military catastrophe in Soviet history. Most likely successor: Molotov, Beria, or Zhukov gaining emergency powers. Possible German-Soviet armistice negotiations (Hitler had not yet committed to full extermination policy in all cases). Or: new Soviet leadership actually performs better — the purged officers had been the most competent.
Second-Order Effects (low confidence)
This is a genuine fork point. A separate peace would have freed Germany to focus on Britain and potentially the Atlantic; it might have prolonged the war in the West dramatically. Alternatively, a collective Soviet leadership might have been more militarily rational (no Stalinist interference in military decisions), potentially accelerating the eventual German defeat. The Holocaust's scale might have been different; Soviet territorial arrangements post-war would certainly have differed. This is low-confidence territory — the range of plausible outcomes is genuinely wide.
Magnitude Assessment
Essential to near-essential for certain outcomes: Stalin's personal decisions (rejecting early warnings, refusing retreats in 1941, the pact with Hitler) were causally critical in ways that structural analysis alone cannot capture. This is one of the cases where individual leadership genuinely mattered at the margin.
